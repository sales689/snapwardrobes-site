How to design a walk-in wardrobe (and 7 mistakes that waste space)

Layouts, aisle widths and drawer heights that work, plus the mistakes we see most often when we measure up Sydney homes.

Design Guide · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Most walk-in wardrobes that disappoint are not too small. They were designed around the room instead of around the clothes. A few hundred millimetres in the wrong place is the difference between a space you enjoy and one you squeeze through every morning.

This guide covers how to plan a walk-in properly, and the seven mistakes we see most often. If you want pricing first, start with our walk-in wardrobe cost guide.

Start with what you own, not the room

Before any layout, take stock. How many dresses and coats need long hanging? How many shirts and jackets can share a double rail? How much is folded, and how many pairs of shoes? A walk-in designed around a real inventory uses every millimetre. One designed around the empty room guesses.

Pick a layout that suits the room width

  • Single run or L-shape: best for narrower rooms. An L-shape is the safest choice under about 1.8m wide.
  • Galley (two parallel runs): needs about 2.1m or more. Hanging cabinetry takes around 600mm each side, and you still want a clear aisle between them.
  • U-shape: works from about 1.8m wide and gives the most storage per square metre, but feels tight if the room is narrower.
  • Island: only worth it in a room with the footprint of a small spare bedroom. Otherwise it blocks the aisle.

The measurement that matters most

Keep a clear walkway of 900mm to 1,000mm. Narrower and you cannot open a drawer while someone walks past. If the room cannot give you that, a built-in wardrobe is usually the better answer than a cramped walk-in.

The 7 mistakes that waste space

1. A walkway that is too narrow

The most common one. It looks fine on a plan, then a drawer pulled open blocks the aisle. Plan for 900mm to 1,000mm of clear space, measured after the drawers are open.

2. Too much long hanging

Most clothing is short. Shirts, jackets, folded trousers and skirts fit a double rail, which doubles your rail space in the same width. Long hanging should be reserved for dresses and coats.

3. Designing around the room, not the clothes

A layout that fills every wall looks efficient, but if it puts folded knits where you need hanging, it fails in daily use. Start with the inventory, then fit it to the walls.

4. Fixed shelves everywhere

Wardrobes change. Adjustable shelving lets you reset the space when the seasons, kids or clothes change, without calling anyone back.

5. No plan for lighting

Interior spaces without a window need light you can actually see by. LED strip lighting is far easier and cleaner when the power and cabling are planned at design stage than when it is added afterwards.

6. Dead corners

Corners are where space disappears. A good design either uses them with a corner unit or turns the run so the corner is not wasted. Ask how each corner will be used before you sign off a layout.

7. Drawers that clash with doors, skirting or power points

Drawers need room to open. A drawer that hits a door frame, a skirting board or a power point wastes the unit it sits in. This is exactly the sort of detail that on-site measuring catches.

Drawer heights that actually work

As a guide, internal heights of roughly:

  • 120mm to 160mm: underwear, socks, jewellery
  • 180mm to 220mm: t-shirts, gym wear
  • 250mm to 350mm: knits, jumpers, handbags

Internal depth of about 450mm to 500mm suits most clothing. Soft-close runners on the drawers you use every day are the upgrade that gets noticed most.

Frequently asked

How wide should a walk-in wardrobe aisle be?

Aim for a clear walkway of 900mm to 1,000mm between the two runs of cabinetry. Hanging cabinetry needs about 600mm of depth on each side, so a galley layout needs a room roughly 2.1m wide or more.

What is the best layout for a small walk-in wardrobe?

An L-shape or a single run usually works best in narrower rooms. A U-shape needs a room about 1.8m wide or more to avoid feeling cramped, and an island needs the footprint of a small spare bedroom.

How much hanging space do I actually need?

Less long hanging than most people expect. Shirts, jackets, skirts and folded trousers fit double hanging, which doubles the rail space. Keep long hanging for dresses and coats, and measure what you own before the layout is set.

How deep should walk-in wardrobe drawers be?

Internal drawer depth of about 450mm to 500mm suits most clothing. Internal heights of roughly 120mm to 160mm suit underwear and jewellery, 180mm to 220mm suit t-shirts and gym wear, and 250mm to 350mm suit knits, jumpers and handbags.

Do I need lighting in a walk-in wardrobe?

Yes, especially without a window. Plan LED strip lighting at design stage so the power point and cabling are in the right place, rather than adding it later.

Is a walk-in wardrobe worth it in a small room?

Not always. If the room is too tight to leave a clear walkway, a well-designed built-in wardrobe gives more usable storage than a cramped walk-in. We will tell you honestly at the consultation which suits your room.
